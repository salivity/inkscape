Laser Kerf Offset for Interlocking Parts in Inkscape

Achieving a snug friction fit for interlocking laser-cut parts requires accounting for the laser kerf—the width of material the beam vaporizes during cutting. This guide explains how to calculate the kerf offset and apply directional adjustments to both interior and exterior paths in Inkscape. By expanding exterior tabs and contracting interior slots by half the kerf width, your assembled components will fit together accurately without being too loose or requiring excessive force.

Understanding and Calculating Kerf Offset

The laser kerf (\(K\)) is the total thickness of the cut line, typically ranging from 0.08 mm to 0.25 mm depending on your laser wattage, lens focal length, speed, and material.

Because the laser beam travels along the center of your vector line, it removes material equally from both sides of the cut:

\[\text{Kerf Offset} = \frac{\text{Measured Kerf}}{2}\]

To find your exact kerf:

  1. Cut a rectangle of a known width (e.g., 50 mm) containing 9 interior vertical cuts, creating 10 equal slats.
  2. Push the cut slats tightly together and measure their total combined width with digital calipers.
  3. Subtract the combined width from the original 50 mm, then divide the difference by 10 (the total number of cut lines).
  4. Divide that result by 2 to find your offset per edge.

Interior vs. Exterior Path Rules

When a laser cuts a shape, it makes the outside dimensions slightly smaller and holes or cutouts slightly larger. To counteract this:

  • Exterior Paths (Tabs, Pins, Outer Perimeters): Must be expanded outward by adding the kerf offset. This ensures the cut edge ends up at the intended dimension instead of shrinking.
  • Interior Paths (Slots, Mortises, Holes): Must be contracted inward by subtracting the kerf offset. This prevents cutouts from becoming too large for the mating pieces.

Method 1: Using Global Inset and Outset Steps

Inkscape allows you to define a precise step distance for the built-in Inset and Outset tools.

  1. Go to Edit > Preferences (or Ctrl + Shift + P).
  2. Navigate to Behavior > Steps.
  3. Locate Inset/Outset by and enter your calculated kerf offset value (e.g., 0.075 mm). Ensure the unit dropdown is set to millimeters or inches matching your document.
  4. Close the Preferences window.
  5. Select the exterior shape or tab path and click Path > Outset (Shortcut: Ctrl + )). The boundary expands outward by your kerf offset.
  6. Select the interior slot or hole path and click Path > Inset (Shortcut: Ctrl + (). The cutout shrinks inward by your kerf offset.

Note: If your slots and outer perimeter are combined into a single compound path (Ctrl + K), you must break them apart (Path > Break Apart) before applying Inset or Outset so the operations apply in the correct directions.

Method 2: Using the Offset Path Effect for Non-Destructive Editing

For complex files where you want non-destructive, numerically controlled offsets:

  1. Select the path you want to modify.
  2. Open the Path Effects panel via Path > Path Effects.
  3. Click the + button to add an effect, and select Offset.
  4. In the effect parameters:
    • For Exterior paths, set the Offset value to positive kerf offset (e.g., 0.075 mm).
    • For Interior paths, set the Offset value to negative kerf offset (e.g., -0.075 mm).
  5. Choose the Join type (Miter, Round, or Bevel) to prevent distorted corners.
  6. Once finalized, convert the effect into a standard vector path by selecting Path > Object to Path.
