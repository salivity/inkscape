Inkscape Affine Matrix for Rotation and Shear

This article explains how Inkscape computes the 2D affine transformation matrix when combining rotation and shear operations on vector objects. It covers the underlying mathematical representation using SVG-compliant transformation matrices, the role of Inkscape's 2Geom geometry engine, the derivation of individual rotation and shear components, and how matrix multiplication order determines the final composite matrix.

The 2D Affine Representation in Inkscape

Inkscape represents all two-dimensional linear transformations as affine matrices matching the SVG standard. In homogeneous coordinates, a point \((x, y)\) is transformed to \((x', y')\) using a \(3 \times 3\) matrix:

\[\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & c & e \\ b & d & f \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

In SVG syntax and Inkscape's internal codebase, this is stored as six scalar values: matrix(a, b, c, d, e, f). The parameters \(a, b, c,\) and \(d\) govern linear transformations (scaling, rotation, and shearing), while \(e\) and \(f\) handle translation along the horizontal and vertical axes.

Under the hood, Inkscape utilizes the Geom::Affine class provided by its computational geometry library, lib2geom. This class encapsulates matrix operations and guarantees numerical stability during complex transformations.

Individual Matrix Components

To combine rotation and shear, Inkscape first defines each operation as a discrete affine matrix.

1. Pure Rotation Matrix

A pure rotation by an angle \(\theta\) around the coordinate origin \((0, 0)\) is defined as:

\[R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

When rotating around an arbitrary center point \((x_0, y_0)\), Inkscape translates the origin to that point, applies the rotation, and translates back:

\[R_{(x_0, y_0)}(\theta) = T(x_0, y_0) \times R(\theta) \times T(-x_0, -y_0)\]

Where \(T(t_x, t_y)\) represents the translation matrix:

\[T(t_x, t_y) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

2. Pure Shear Matrix

Shear (skewing) distorts an object along one axis relative to the other.

A horizontal shear by an angle \(\alpha\) (or factor \(k_x = \tan\alpha\)) shifts coordinates along the X-axis:

\[S_x(\alpha) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tan\alpha & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

A vertical shear by an angle \(\beta\) (or factor \(k_y = \tan\beta\)) shifts coordinates along the Y-axis:

\[S_y(\beta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \tan\beta & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

A general shear combining both angles is computed by multiplying individual shear matrices:

\[S(\alpha, \beta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tan\alpha & 0 \\ \tan\beta & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

As with rotation, if the shearing is performed around an active transformation center \((x_0, y_0)\), translation matrices are wrapped around the shear matrix: \(T(x_0, y_0) \times S \times T(-x_0, -y_0)\).

Computing the Combined Transformation

Matrix multiplication is non-commutative (\(A \times B \neq B \times A\)). Therefore, the order in which rotation and shear are applied directly dictates the resulting matrix values.

When a transformation is applied incrementally via the UI, Inkscape post-multiplies the new transformation matrix by the object's existing matrix. If an object is first sheared and then rotated, the composite transformation matrix \(M_{total}\) is:

\[M_{total} = R(\theta) \times S(\alpha, \beta)\]

Expanding this product produces:

\[M_{total} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tan\alpha & 0 \\ \tan\beta & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

Carrying out the matrix multiplication gives the combined matrix components:

  • \(a = \cos\theta - \tan\beta \sin\theta\)
  • \(b = \sin\theta + \tan\beta \cos\theta\)
  • \(c = \tan\alpha \cos\theta - \sin\theta\)
  • \(d = \tan\alpha \sin\theta + \cos\theta\)

If translation offsets exist due to an off-center transformation pivot \((x_0, y_0)\), the full chain expands to:

\[M_{total} = T(x_0, y_0) \times R(\theta) \times S(\alpha, \beta) \times T(-x_0, -y_0)\]

The translation terms \(e\) and \(f\) accumulate the resulting positional offsets:

  • \(e = x_0 - a \cdot x_0 - c \cdot y_0\)
  • \(f = y_0 - b \cdot x_0 - d \cdot y_0\)

Storage and Application in the Document

Once the combined values \([a, b, c, d, e, f]\) are computed via lib2geom, Inkscape decides how to store them based on user preferences:

  1. Optimized Mode: Inkscape decomposes the matrix and directly modifies the coordinate definitions of the path nodes whenever mathematically possible without distorting primitive attributes.
  2. Preserved Mode: Inkscape preserves the original object geometry and writes the computed composite values directly into the element's XML attribute as transform="matrix(a, b, c, d, e, f)".
